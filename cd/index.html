As artists and craftspeople we set our intention before we pick up our tools. For creative direction that's tone, mood, theme, and message. What are we making, what experience are we trying to capture, and what is its intended impact on the audience? On Ghost, this was very much the first step "We want to make you feel like you're playing a Kurosawa film." Everything else built from that.

It's easy to get lost in what you want to make — or worse, to put your craft above the player. The discipline is holding the game vision in one hand and player desire in the other. On City of Heroes the community was practically embedded on the team (highly active forums where devs would have direct communication with them, personal meet ups multiple times a year, even content development with a system called Mission Architect I led).

With intention and player expectation aligned, you can build toward the player experience. This is more than systems, art, or design in isolation. Those are each sparks that can light the fire of a game. On Diablo 3, we already new what players loved about the world and gameplay. My mission as Narrative Lead was to both push the world forward, while holding tightly to what made it great to begin with.

As a Creative Director, you are not the artist, the engineer, or the writer. Instead, you have to hold this idea in your head that few others can see, like a distant mountain top you need to get everyone else to see. You don't know the path through the forest. You didn't bring a torch for the caves, or a boat for the river. Instead, you depend on everyone else on the team to find the path, light the torch, and build the boat. Your job is clarity of destination and to trust them to do the rest.

Repeat the vision. Do it in meetings, in documents, in hallway conversations, and to yourself. Creative direction doesn't land once — it has to land a thousand times in a dozen different ways. Develop with intention, cultivate player expectation, capture the experience, and give clarity to a team smarter than you. Then do it all again.

IV — Homework One
How do you tell compelling stories in a multiplayer space?

Four Layers.
Always Running.

While there are likely many ways to do this and it all really depends on what you want your players to experience, here's an example of how I could approach this. From the overall shape of the world and the story that is emerging from everything below it, to the community that's driving the direction, the events that force collisions, challenge, and chaos, to each players personal journey and motivations, and finally, the game story itself that acts as a backdrop and a back stop for the player experience - it all builds on top of itself to create a compelling, multiplayer story. That is Runeterra.

V — Homework Two
How does the game generate UNSCRIPTED memorable moments?

Surprise Can't Be Scripted

At dusk, a meteor falls from the sky. Wildlife flees into surrounding areas and villages creating havoc. Scavengers seek the hidden mysteries within the celestial body, while others surround the area waiting.

These are not specifically scripted experiences, but the way this plays out could be a story some players tell for years. My job is to demand a world where this keeps happening. Design's job is to build the collision that makes it inevitable. 

VI — Homework Three
How does the world function as a character that players belong to?

A World That Acts
and Reacts.

The world shows players what they've done to it — and responds. Small choices that can compound into bigger ones. Over the course of a single session or over weeks of play with allies, you can see the shape of the world based off of player's impact. In every case, the world reacts.

Player Action → World Reaction → Player Response
VII — Dream Big
WHERE i COULD SEE MMOS HEADING

Big Dreams of a
Future MMO.

I
Offerings to the Gods

Give players the power to decide what happens to the world through more than combat. Not a voting UI — diegetic ritual. Freljord players offer to the Three Sisters. Shuriman players petition the Ascended. Accumulated offerings shape the next live update. Players steer the world. The live team gets a real signal. Neither side breaks the frame.

II
THE SHIFTING TIDES OF THE WORLD

The world should never let a victory be final or a defeat be the end. Loss and Dominance are a wheel everyone moves around. One can build momentum while the other feels dangerous. Players should read the state of the world in what they see, not in a UI. This connects them emotionally to the world and the systems.

III
A Life Within The Game

Create a system where players feel as if there is a story they are taking part in, rather than just moving through. Play solo for years and never run out. Hunt bounties. Run economic espionage. Build a house. Run a harbor. The same world — radically different lives. The ambition is the sheer depth of content required to make all of it real at once.

Framework 02

Fantasy to Content

The idea here is to start with the high level concept of the game "A LoL MMO that takes palce on Runterra", then to drill down into the Player Fantasy we're offering in that game, describe what that fantasy is, what acctions support it, and finally what content needs to be created to reinforce it. Every action, mechanic, and content type traces back to that description. If it doesn't — it doesn't belong.

Systems Collision

The Untelegraphed Moment

Three independent systems — day/night cycle, roaming enemies, a meteor event — fire simultaneously. No designer planned the outcome. Players generated it. This is the model.

Living World Engine

Dynamic World State — Freljord

Player action moves the state. The state determines what exists in the world at any moment — NPCs, quests, weather, champions, events. Extreme dominance in either direction triggers a Void surge. Balance can never hold forever. That's the drama.

Framework 03

Bringing Creative Direction to Life

Four artifacts that translate creative direction from concept into something a team can feel, reference, and build from.

Story Pitch

A 20-page document: 10 pages of found art that captures the world's visual feeling, 10 pages of text covering what's unique, the genre, key characters, and what this game is a love letter to. The first artifact that makes the game feel real to a team that hasn't seen it yet.

Reference example: Stranger Things series bible.

Tone Video

Found media — film, anime, TV — cut to a piece of music that captures the world's emotional register. 2–3 minutes. The fastest way to align a team on tone before a word of design is written.
